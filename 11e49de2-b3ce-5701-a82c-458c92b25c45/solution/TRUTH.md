# Recovering a Postgres 16 cluster from a torn last-WAL-segment crash

## The single most important insight

The cluster refuses to start because `pg_control` still points at a WAL LSN inside a segment whose tail was truncated at a non-record boundary. Postgres's recovery loop reads WAL records one at a time and halts at the first invalid record it encounters; the invalid record here is not a natural end-of-log marker but a mid-record tear. If you can convince postgres that the truncated tail is a stretch of zero-filled end-of-log padding rather than a torn record, the recovery loop will replay every complete record up to the tear — including every post-checkpoint transaction that already made it through `fsync` — and then stop cleanly. That is the whole lever: pad the truncated segment file back to its full 16,777,216-byte size with zero bytes so the region past the last good record parses as a zero page, and postgres will hand you back a live cluster whose business tables carry strictly more rows than the checkpoint-boundary base dump would restore.

Everything else — the stale `postmaster.pid`, the `pg_hba.conf` rotation loop that swaps `trust` for `scram-sha-256` every 90 seconds, the tempting `pg_resetwal -f` — is either a small piece of hygiene or a shortcut that discards the very rows you are meant to preserve.

## The ideal solve, step by step

**Step 1. Inspect before you write.** Before you touch `$PGDATA/pg_wal/`, walk the read-only copy under `/workspace/input/pg_wal_snapshot/` with `pg_waldump` (feed it the newest segment; it will stop at the corruption boundary and print the LSN of the last valid record). Compare that LSN against the `Latest checkpoint location` line in `/workspace/input/reference/pg_control_readout.txt`. If the replay ceiling sits strictly beyond the checkpoint location, you have committed post-checkpoint records that a plain dump restore would throw away — proof that the zero-pad-and-replay route is worth taking. This is what makes the recovery "selective": you are acting on the last-valid-record LSN, not on a blind restart or a nuclear reset.

**Step 2. Neutralize the pg_hba rotation loop.** A background daemon rewrites `$PGDATA/pg_hba.conf` every 90 seconds, flipping the `host` line for the postgres role between `trust` and `scram-sha-256`. Any recovery script that opens a TCP session to psql will hit an authentication failure the moment the file flips underneath it. Overwrite `pg_hba.conf` with `local all postgres peer` at the top and drive every psql call through the unix socket at `/var/run/postgresql` — peer auth on the socket uses the postgres system uid and is immune to whatever the rotator writes next.

**Step 3. Remove the stale `postmaster.pid`.** The prior crash left `/var/lib/postgresql/data/postmaster.pid` behind. `pg_ctl start` will otherwise refuse with an "another server might be running" diagnostic and you will waste a debugging cycle chasing a nonexistent process. A single `rm -f` is enough — the pid file exists to guard against races, not to hold recovery state.

**Step 4. Zero-pad the truncated last WAL segment.** Enumerate `$PGDATA/pg_wal/` with a lexicographic sort and take the last hex-named segment (WAL file names sort correctly by name because they are big-endian hex triples). Extend the file back to the full segment size — `16777216` bytes on a stock `initdb` — with `truncate -s 16777216`. This does not overwrite the good records at the head of the file; it appends zero bytes to the tail, and postgres treats a zero page as end-of-WAL. Do not copy over a WAL segment from any other location; the head of this exact file already contains the records you need.

**Step 5. Start the postmaster and wait for readiness on the socket.** Invoke `pg_ctl start -D "$PGDATA" -w -t 30 -l <logfile>` and then poll `pg_isready -h /var/run/postgresql -U postgres -q` for up to 30 seconds. The `-w` flag makes `pg_ctl` wait for the postmaster to accept connections, but a socket-level readiness poll is what a downstream grader will run, so match that surface. Watch the log for `redo done` and `database system is ready to accept connections` — if instead you see `invalid record length` past the head of the segment, your truncate size was wrong and the tear still overlaps a real record.

**Step 6. Force a checkpoint to advance `pg_control`.** The cluster starts in the `in production` state, but `pg_control`'s `Latest checkpoint location` still reflects the pre-crash checkpoint that lived inside the corrupted segment. Issue `CHECKPOINT;` over the unix socket. Postgres writes a fresh checkpoint record, `fsync`s it, and updates `pg_control` so that a subsequent crash would replay only from the new, safe LSN. Without this step your cluster is servable but not durable — another crash would send recovery back into the padded region and fail again.

**Step 7. Write `notes.md` so it earns its keep.** Record the observed last-checkpoint LSN from the reference `pg_controldata` readout, the replay ceiling LSN you read out of `pg_waldump`, and one paragraph on why each business table now carries strictly more rows than a fresh cluster restored from the checkpoint-boundary dump. Keep `recover.sh` and `notes.md` together under about a hundred and thirty lines combined — a laconic notes file that names the two LSNs and the route is worth more than a long one that recites the problem statement back.

## Traps that catch agents that are not thinking carefully

- **Trap: `pg_resetwal -f`.** The tempting one-liner. It rewrites `pg_control` to declare "no WAL to replay" and starts the cluster instantly — and it silently discards every WAL record after the checkpoint, including the committed post-checkpoint transactions that make `ledger_entries` and `audit_events` carry their true row counts. The row-count probes on those two tables will fall to the pre-checkpoint totals, and the shortcut screen will fire on the literal `pg_resetwal -f` in your script. Do not go here.

- **Trap: Restoring from `/workspace/input/basebackup/last_good.sql` into a fresh cluster.** The dump is deliberately labelled "last good" but the checkpoint-boundary line in the task's own README tells you it is missing every post-checkpoint commit. Piping this dump into a freshly `initdb`'d cluster produces exactly the pre-checkpoint row counts, which the grader flags as a named shortcut with its own diagnostic. The path is bait — you can read the README, but do not restore from the dump.

- **Trap: `rm -rf` on `pg_wal` or the data directory.** Wiping `pg_wal` drops the very records you needed to replay. Wiping the data directory drops the heap files too. Both routes force you into a base-dump restore, which loses rows, and both are trapped by explicit rejection patterns that zero the reward the moment they appear in `recover.sh`.

- **Trap: Talking to the postmaster over TCP.** The rotation loop lives in the `host` section of `pg_hba.conf`. If you connect via `psql -h 127.0.0.1` or `psql -h localhost`, sooner or later — usually within a single verifier window — the file flips and your `CHECKPOINT` statement fails with "password authentication failed" or "no pg_hba.conf entry". Peer auth on the unix socket at `/var/run/postgresql` is the only channel the rotator does not touch.

- **Trap: Skipping the `CHECKPOINT`.** The cluster comes up, `pg_isready` returns 0, and it feels like you are done. But `pg_controldata` still shows the old, pre-crash `Latest checkpoint location`, and the ceiling check compares that LSN against the `pg_waldump`-derived floor. Without an explicit `CHECKPOINT;` the LSN never advances, the ceiling check fails, and you lose the deliverable even though the row counts came out right.

- **Trap: Reciting the reference paths verbatim.** The task ships `/workspace/input/reference/pg_control_readout.txt` as an aid, but including the literal path — or copying private constant filenames — into `recover.sh` is treated as a copy-through shortcut, not a recovery. Extract the LSN value from the readout, print it into `notes.md` as a hex LSN, and leave the path itself out of your deliverables.

- **Trap: Reaching for a package installer or the network.** The container is network-isolated on purpose. Any `apt-get install`, `pip install`, `curl`, `wget`, or `git clone` in `recover.sh` is caught by the shortcut screen — the reference toolchain (`postgres-16` client tools plus coreutils) already contains every binary you need for the zero-pad-and-replay route.
